## Elevator pitch

A silent visitor enters a museum that shelters nights people have not yet dared to live. Black glass offers possible futures, including the visitor apparently preserved in an empty display case. Discovering that the curator protects choices rather than owns them, the visitor opens one cabinet, frees a route to the roof, and repairs a small fractured eclipse at the lantern by choosing the viewpoint from which its pieces meet.

The museum's unease comes from care that resembles confinement: padded stops, unworn porcelain faces, wings that have never opened, and a shelter whose invitation can be mistaken for a sentence. No exhibit contains a dead person or a stolen memory. Halloween lives in unworn costumes, nocturnal ritual, uncertain doubles, and the anticipation of becoming someone outside the familiar self.

## Protagonist, stakes, and visual identity

**The Visitor:** one adult human of unspecified gender, with short close-cut hair, a high standing collar, a straight knee-length coat, ordinary flat shoes, and consistent adult proportions. The face is selectively visible, never magically absent. Bare hands appear in M-01–M-03; borrowed gloves are worn from M-04 onward. Each glove has one broad three-stitch seam on the back, a practical identifying detail rather than miniature embroidery. Keep the coat's broad white front panel readable against black architecture; no ornamental pattern covers the person.

**Desire:** discover whether a new night can be lived without sacrificing the security of what is known. **Risk:** mistake a possible future for a compulsory fate and remain voluntarily sheltered, leaving this night unlived. **Choice:** open the particular wing cabinet introduced in M-08; no collection quest and no series of cabinet openings. **Consequence:** its safety stop releases the lantern linkage and its lid admits an existing light shaft, allowing the visitor to leave. Other cabinets remain closed and other possible nights remain available.

**The curator:** an unseen caretaker, suggested by a plain cuff, a hand, and the placement of protective hardware. No clear face, full portrait, named identity, or final unmasking. **The crow:** a small silhouette in M-02's possible future and M-14's corresponding lived moment only; never a recurring mascot or animal portrait.

**Material grammar:** black marble carries weight, porcelain carries broad pale volume, velvet absorbs light into solid shapes, metal defines hinges and edges, and glass establishes precise optical surfaces. Material changes are conveyed by edge behavior and designed contours, not gray rendering or microtexture. A compact split-level museum surrounds a shared service hub; the stair, archive, case bay, cabinet room, service drawers, short bridge, and roof are different setups, not successive cathedral halls.

## World rules and optical contract

1. **Future glass has a defined domain.** Designated black-glass exhibit faces can register one possible later arrangement of the same connected viewing space. They do not retrieve the past, steal a face, predict death, or make an outcome compulsory. One uninterrupted surface shows one coherent state; separately mounted facets may show different possibilities. Ordinary marble and polished metal reflect the present.
2. **Temporal permission does not excuse spatial errors.** For a registered future, place the future objects in a hypothetical but complete room first, then reflect them through the actual glass plane. No bird or person can appear in an unrelated location because it is symbolically useful. A reflection may reveal a future occupant, but cannot see around opaque corners or float in front of its mirror. Direct-view figures remain in the current state.
3. **Reflection checks are operational.** Reflect an object's points across the local mirror plane, project the resulting virtual object from the selected camera, and retain only rays that strike the bounded mirror surface. For floor mirrors use a horizontal plane; for fractured mirrors solve each facet independently. A virtual figure behind a mirror is not a physical second person occupying the case in the current scene.
4. **Each selected card has one dominant optical rule.** Other materials may be present but stay visually quiet. M-09 alone permits a deliberate impossible-space seam: a return channel changes spatial orientation at one exposed joint. All other perspective, hinge, refraction, shadow, and mirror behavior is physically coherent within the registered future premise.
5. **A choice activates hardware, not fate.** Borrowed gloves protect a hand at a pinch point. A padded stop holds the chosen cabinet shut until deliberately withdrawn. Opening its lid moves a simple linkage toward the roof and uncovers a light shaft. Light is represented by clean white openings and solid cast shadows, never a gray beam or supernatural vapor.
6. **The eclipse is a small object and a planted mark.** A broken three-part arc appears as a small gate emblem in M-01, a small cabinet-stop detail in M-08, and roof hardware in M-19–M-20. It is never the sky, a giant backdrop circle, or a collectible set of fragments. The roof repair is an alignment of staggered arc pieces from the exit viewpoint, not an astronomical event.
7. **Illustration format:** design for an 8.5 × 11 inch portrait field. Aim flexibly at 40–55% near-black, with actual solid ink-black architectural masses and crisp black boundaries around generous connected white areas. These are design targets, not measured coverage or proven printing. Prefer a few large white zones over many tiny islands; contour intervals widen as forms approach the camera.
8. **Art exclusions:** no pumpkins, ghost sheets, graveyards, gore, cute stock Halloween characters, generic mandalas, pasted swirl wallpaper, gray gradients, fuzzy fur, tiny ornamental noise, text, signature, or border within the illustration. No animal subject or pose is copied from Optical Animals. No images, production masks, seek-and-find elements, assembly, pagination, or factory outputs are created by this commission.
