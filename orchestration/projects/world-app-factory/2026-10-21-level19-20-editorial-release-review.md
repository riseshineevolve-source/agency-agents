**E1 - L19 p82_b01.text**

> (Shaking one hand very logically) We change direction. Like in basketball. Plan: 'Indoor Beach'. I am taking charge of the planning. I've drawn a giant sun and found our beach towels. The weather department has been replaced by crayons. Alio, I need you to design the shape of the towel-beach so no one kicks each other.

The safety assessment is an inference from the described imitation opportunity
and official appliance guidance. The source does not specify the heater type
or appliance clearances; this review does not claim that a particular fire
outcome is proven.

**E2 - L19 p84_b01.heading**

> THE BRAIN SECRET (When Plans Change)

**E2 - L19 p84_b01.science**

> When an exciting plan changes, disappointment can feel big and uncomfortable. Children are still learning how to handle strong feelings. Naming the feeling and getting support can help before you work out a new plan.

**E3 - L19 p84_b03.science**

> An exciting plan can feel like a promise. When it changes, acknowledge the disappointment and explain what happened.

**E4 - L19 p85_b02.body**

> Why did Mimi start jumping and shaking? Sometimes movement helps us handle tense feelings. If you want to, try a little Shake-Out Dance in a clear space, or choose a quieter pause. You do not have to feel better instantly. When you are ready, ask your Captain to help you find the next plan.

This is an optional coping suggestion, not a treatment claim or a promise that
one movement works for every child.

**E5 - L20 p91_b01.heading**

> THE BRAIN SECRET (Pictures Are Not Proof)

**E5 - L20 p91_b01.science**

> A convincing picture can still be invented or shown with a misleading caption. Looking real is not enough. Pause and check where it came from and whether trustworthy sources support the claim.

**E6 - L20 p91_b02.science**

> How to check a surprising picture?
>
> - Look: Odd details can be clues, but they do not prove a picture is AI-generated. A realistic picture can be invented too.
> - Source: Find the original post. Check who made it, when, and what it actually says.
> - Check: Ask your Captain to help compare the claim with reliable sources before sharing.

**E6 - L20 p89_b02.text**

> (Standing at the head of the table, turning on her red laser pointer and acting like a teacher) Those blurry spots and human-looking fingernails make me suspicious, noob. But odd details are clues, not proof. Let's check who posted this and whether we can find the original video.

**E7 - L20 p92_b02.body, recommended**

> False stories can spread far and fast online. Before passing one on, check who posted it and what evidence supports it. If a post makes you feel shocked or angry, pause and ask your Captain to help you check. A strong feeling is not proof that a story is true.

**E7 - optional scoped statistic, only if separately approved**

> In one study of stories shared on Twitter from 2006 to 2017, true stories took about six times as long as false stories to reach 1,500 people. This describes that study, not every post.

The original study examined fact-checked rumor cascades and their diffusion;
its six-times comparison concerns time to reach 1,500 users. It did not measure
amygdala activity or establish nervous-system causation. Do not attach that
causal explanation to the scoped statistic. [S8]
